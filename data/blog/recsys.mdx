## Introduction

i built a recommendation model for a video app because i wanted to actually understand recsys **(Recommendation System)**, not just call a managed API and nod along.

at first it looked easy:

- train a ranker
- compute NDCG
- number go up, ship it

then reality happened. the number kept going up in ways that felt too good. turns out the thing lying to me wasn't the model. it was my own evaluation code

## The Two-Tower Detour

before any of the eval drama, i made a bigger mistake first: model choice.

i'd seen netflix and tiktok talk about two-tower neural networks for retrieval, so that's what i built. user tower, item tower, embeddings, dot product. felt like the "real" recsys architecture, the one actual big companies use.

it barely beat random.

took me longer than i'd like to admit to see why: netflix and tiktok train two-tower models on hundreds of millions to billions of interactions. i had a few thousand. there wasn't enough signal for the embeddings to learn anything structured, so they just collapsed toward noise, mostly re-deriving popularity through a much more expensive path.

i dropped the two-tower idea and looked at gradient-boosted trees instead, since they're known to work well on small, tabular data. still had to pick one: AdaBoost, XGBoost, or LightGBM.

- [**AdaBoost**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AdaBoost): oldest of the three, reweights misclassified examples each round. simple, but weaker on this kind of ranking task and slower to get right.
- [**XGBoost**](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/): the popular default, strong and well-documented, but heavier to set up and slower to train than i needed for a project this size.
- [**LightGBM**](https://lightgbm.readthedocs.io/): also gradient-boosted trees, but grows leaf-wise instead of level-wise, so it trains faster and uses less memory on the same data. it also has a built-in `lambdarank` objective, made exactly for ranking problems like this one.

went with LightGBM. fast to train, small enough to iterate on quickly, and the ranking objective was already there instead of something i'd have to bolt on.

why this mattered:

- copying a company's model architecture without copying their data scale is copying the wrong variable
- neural embeddings need volume to be worth their variance. small data plus a high-capacity model gets you memorization or noise, not generalization
- when picking between similar tools, the one that lets you train and iterate fastest usually wins early on, you can always swap it later once you actually know what you need

mindset shift: "what does netflix use" is the wrong question when you don't have netflix's data. the right question is "what works with the data i actually have."

## What I Built (Simple Version)

three moving parts:

- features: popularity, watch history, tag overlap, user/video stats
- model: LightGBM with a lambdarank objective
- eval: per-user recall@k / NDCG@k on a time-based split (train on older data, validate on the newest slice)

that last part sounds boring. it is where all the actual story happened.

## Where It Got Real

### 1. Early Stopping on the Wrong Metric

when tuning a model, you need something to decide "stop training now, you're done."
i used LightGBM's built-in pairwise NDCG for that. seemed reasonable. it was not.

the built-in metric peaked after about three boosting rounds at a high learning rate, so every "tuned" model the search produced was three trees deep, and the tuner just kept cranking the learning rate to its ceiling because that's what the (wrong) metric rewarded.

this produced a couple of really exciting scores that were, on reflection, complete nonsense.

why this mattered:

- optimizing metric A while reporting metric B means you're not actually tuning what you think you're tuning
- a metric that peaks suspiciously fast is a metric worth interrogating, not celebrating
- the fix was a custom callback that evaluates the _real_ dedup NDCG every 10 rounds and stops on that, instead of trusting the builtin

mindset shift: your early-stopping metric and your reporting metric have to be the same metric, or you're tuning blind.

### 2. Measuring Time and Position, Not Just Counts

at first my features were basic: video metadata, watch history, raw view count, tag overlap from search. simple counts, no sense of _when_ or _where_ those counts applied.

problem: a video's live `view_count` is a running total as of today, not as of the moment a given user actually saw it. training on that leaks the future into the past, the feature already "knows" popularity the interaction couldn't have known yet. worse, a brand-new video reads as 0 views forever off that live column, so it stays buried by the popularity feature no matter how good it turns out to be.

fix: switched to point-in-time features. instead of the live cumulative `view_count`, i built `video_view_count_pit`, the number of views strictly before that interaction happened, computed per interaction off timestamped view history. same idea for exposure position: `rank_in_surface` records exactly where a video sat in the feed when it was shown, so i know whether it was ranked #1 or buried at #40, not just that it was shown at all.

why this mattered:

- a feature computed "as of today" instead of "as of the moment that matters" is a leak, even when it looks like a normal aggregate
- point-in-time counts fix cold start too: a new video isn't stuck at 0 forever, it's scored on however many views it genuinely had by that point
- `rank_in_surface` separates "this video failed because no one wanted it" from "this video failed because it was buried and barely seen" - two different problems that look identical without position data

### 3. The Popularity Shortcut

even with an honest eval, there was a quieter problem: random negative sampling.

if you sample negatives uniformly from the whole catalog, you're mostly sampling low-view videos, because most videos have low views. so "rank whatever has more views higher" separates positives from negatives almost every time, with zero actual personalization required. the model took that shortcut immediately and became, by feature importance, a popularity ranker wearing a recsys costume.

fix: sample some negatives proportional to view count instead of uniformly, so popular videos land on both sides of the comparison and the model actually has to discriminate between them using something other than "big number good."

net effect: the honest validation score didn't move. but the _reason_ it scored what it scored changed completely, personalization features went from background noise to meaningfully load-bearing. same grade, very different homework.

## Honest vs. Fake: A Timeline

this is the part that still makes me wince a little.

```text
run 1: "great score" -> was optimizing the wrong early-stop metric
run 2: "even better score" -> was exploiting a tie-breaking bug (real score: zero)
run 3: "the number went down" -> this was the first honest one
```

going down is what "actually working" looked like, once the test stopped lying. that's a hard thing to feel good about in the moment, and exactly the thing you have to feel good about anyway.

## 5 Things That Made This Feel Real

### 1. Reliability of the Measurement, Not Just the Model

the boring lesson that saves you every time: your evaluator is part of the system under test.

- fixed early stopping: stop on the metric you actually report, not a convenient proxy.
- fixed tie-breaking: no free wins from identical scores.
- re-ran every previous "good" result through the honest pipeline before trusting any of them.

### 2. Consistency

if "good" can mean different things depending on which code path measured it, nothing is comparable.

- one evaluation function, used everywhere: training, tuning, and reporting.
- no double-crediting: ties get split credit, not full credit for everyone involved.
- re-checked historical checkpoints under the same honest rules instead of grandfathering old numbers in.

### 3. Not Scaling a Bug

more tuning trials just means more chances for a broken metric to find and exploit its own hole faster.

- a hyperparameter search is only as honest as the function it's optimizing against.
- "the tuner found a great config" is not evidence the config is great, it's evidence the tuner is good at its job, whatever that job actually is.

### 4. Catching Yourself Before You Ship

the failure mode here is silent. nothing crashes. nothing throws. the number just looks fantastic.

- treat suspiciously good results as a bug report against your own eval code, not a win to announce yet.
- reproduce the "great" result from scratch before trusting it survives.
- when a model's behavior looks weirdly uniform (same score for everything), go looking for what's rewarding that uniformity.

### 5. Shipping the Boring Number

no fireworks needed for real progress to count.

- an honest, modest score is worth more than an exciting one that doesn't survive a second look
- the model that beat the live heuristic by a real, reproducible margin - not a flashy one, a boring one that held up - is the one that shipped

## Things I Removed From My Thinking

- "a high score means the model understood the task"
- "if the tuner found it automatically, it must be a genuine improvement"
- "a model that performs uniformly well everywhere is a strong, general model"

none of these survive an honest metric.

## What I Kept

- interrogate your evaluation code with the same suspicion you'd apply to the model
- reproduce a "great" result before believing it
- treat a too-perfect score as a bug report, not a milestone
- design your eval to fail loudly, not to accidentally reward laziness

## Conclusion

i started this project to learn recsys. i ended up learning that the eval pipeline deserves as much scrutiny as the model itself, because a broken metric doesn't fail, it just quietly agrees with whatever you did.

the lessons that stuck:

- your early-stopping metric and your reported metric must be the same function
- tie-breaking bugs are free wins for a model, and free wins are a red flag
- a score going down after a fix can be the most honest progress you make all week

that's the part i'm carrying into every model i build next, recsys or otherwise.
